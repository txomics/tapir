1  Why long reads?

NoteIn this chapter we’ll cover…

Code: None to run—this chapter is all concepts, with a short preview of TAPIR’s R syntax at the end.

1.1 One gene, many transcripts

Most human genes produce more than one distinct mature RNA molecule. Rather than acting as a blueprint for a single product, a gene can generate multiple transcript isoforms that differ in several ways from end to end:

  • Where they start (Alternative transcription start sites, TSS): Using different promoters changes the 5′ UTR and sometimes the first coding exon, affecting when the transcript is made and how efficiently it is translated.
  • How they are spliced (Alternative splicing): Including, skipping, or trimming exons reshuffles the coding sequence, which can add or remove entire functional domains from the resulting protein.
  • Where they end (Alternative transcription end sites, TES): Cleaving and polyadenylating the RNA at different sites alters the 3′ UTR, changing where the transcript localizes in the cell and how quickly it is degraded.

Because a single gene can mix and match these choices across thousands of bases, the identity, and resulting biological job, of an isoform depends on its complete, end-to-end combination of start site, splice junctions, and end site. Crucially, cells often shift which isoforms they produce during development, between tissues, or in disease without changing the gene’s total expression level at all.

1.2 What short reads can and can’t tell you

Standard RNA-seq breaks RNA into fragments of a few hundred bases—much shorter than a typical human transcript (around 1,500–2,000 bases). Because a single short-read usually covers only one or two exons, it cannot tell you which distant exons, start sites, or end sites occurred together on the same molecule.

As a result, most short reads are compatible with several isoforms at once. Short-read quantifiers (such as Salmon or kallisto) assign these ambiguous reads to isoforms statistically, but these estimates carry uncertainty, largely depending on the amount of exons shared between a gene’s isoforms.

Short reads are still cheap, generate far more reads per sample, and excel at measuring gene-level expression and individual splice junctions. Many downstream steps in this book apply equally well to short-read transcript counts; look for the Also works with short reads badge.

1.3 What long reads change (and what they don’t)

Long-read platforms sequence thousands of bases from a single cDNA or native RNA molecule, often capturing the entire transcript in one read. By keeping each transcript’s start site, splice junctions, and end site intact on the same read, long reads make isoform identification far more direct, uncover unannotated isoforms, and improve transcript-level quantification.

The two main long-read platforms available as of 2026 differ in ways that affect upstream processing (I think we can improve, or maybe remove, this table:

PacBio (Iso-Seq, Kinnex) Oxford Nanopore (cDNA, direct RNA)
Read accuracy Very high (HiFi consensus reads) Lower historically; much improved in recent chemistries
Throughput High when concatenating molecules (Kinnex); lower for standard Iso-Seq Flexible; scales from portable (MinION) to high-throughput (PromethION)
Special features Strong 5′-to-3′ full-length capture; high-confidence splice junctions Direct RNA sequencing preserves native molecules and RNA modifications

Long reads don’t solve everything, however. There are still limitations that will appear throughout these workflows and shape how we choose to analyze the data:

  • Not every read is full-length. RNA degradation and incomplete reverse transcription produce reads truncated at the 5′ end, which can masquerade as shorter “novel” isoforms.
  • Quantification is still an estimate. Because truncated reads (and isoforms that differ only at their very ends) can still match multiple transcripts, quantifiers such as oarfish and bambu still assign ambiguous reads probabilistically.
  • Technical artifacts can look like biology. Internal priming of the oligo(dT) primer on A-rich stretches of RNA creates false 3′ ends, and small alignment errors near splice sites create false junctions. Structural QC tools like SQANTI3 are essential for flagging these.
  • Fewer reads per sample. Long-read runs typically yield fewer total reads than short-read runs, reducing statistical power for lowly expressed genes and rare isoforms.

1.4 Where TAPIR fits in current analysis pipelines

A long-read transcriptomics project has two phases. The first phase consists of turning raw reads into a transcript catalog and a count matrix. This typically is done via command line tools, and good pipelines are already in place for this (point to?). TAPIR focuses on the second phase: the statistical analysis and biological interpretation you do in R (or Python!) once you have transcript counts.

Stage What happens Example tools What you get
1. Alignment Reads are mapped to the genome (or directly to a transcriptome) minimap2 BAM files
2. Isoform discovery (optional) Novel isoforms are identified from genome alignments alongside known ones bambu, IsoQuant, FLAIR, TALON A GTF of known + novel transcripts
3. Quantification Reads are assigned to transcripts oarfish, bambu, IsoQuant A transcript × sample count matrix
4. Structural QC Discovered transcripts are classified against the reference SQANTI3 A transcript classification table
5. Analysis in R Exploration, testing, and biological interpretation TAPIR workflows Figures, tables, and biological insight

1.4.1 What you’ll bring into R (This section feels misplaced, maybe misnamed)

Most workflows in this book start from three inputs:

  1. A transcript-level count matrix, with transcripts as rows and samples as columns.
  2. A sample table describing each sample’s condition, batch, and other covariates.
  3. A transcript annotation (GTF/GFF) giving the exon coordinates of every reference and newly discovered transcript, often paired with a structural classification table.

If your pipeline included isoform discovery, tools like SQANTI3 (or bambu and TALON, which output similar labels) classify each transcript by how its splice junctions compare to the reference annotation:

Category Meaning Keep in mind
FSM: Full Splice Match Matches all splice junctions of a known reference transcript The highest-confidence category
ISM: Incomplete Splice Match Matches a consecutive subset of a known transcript’s junctions, but is shorter at the 5′ and/or 3′ end Often a 5′-truncated read artifact, though some represent real shorter isoforms
NIC: Novel In Catalog Uses known donor and acceptor splice sites, but in a new combination Often a genuine novel isoform (e.g., a new exon-skipping event)
NNC: Novel Not in Catalog Uses at least one donor or acceptor splice site not in the reference Inspect carefully; can be a true novel exon/site or an alignment artifact

(Note: Different discovery tools sometimes use slightly different names—TALON, for example, calls FSMs “Known”. We point these out where relevant.)

1.5 Four questions you can ask

Once you have transcript-level counts in hand, there are several different questions you can ask:

Question Name Unit of test Example tools
Does the gene’s total output change? Differential gene expression (DGE) Gene DESeq2, edgeR, limma
Does the absolute abundance of an individual transcript change? Differential transcript expression (DTE) Transcript DESeq2, edgeR, fishpond
Does the relative mix of a gene’s isoforms change? Differential transcript usage (DTU) Transcript proportion within its gene satuRn, DRIMSeq, DEXSeq
What structural features separate the isoforms that increase from those that decrease? Splicing / event annotation Exon / splice site / UTR splicelogic

Notice the difference between the middle two rows. DTE asks whether a transcript’s overall abundance changed, which could simply reflect the whole gene turning up or down. DTU, asks whether the proportions of a gene’s isoforms shifted relative to one another.

DTU is where long reads truly shine. Imagine a gene whose total expression stays completely flat between conditions, but whose dominant isoform flips from Isoform A (80% → 20%) to Isoform B (20% → 80%) after treatment. A gene-level DGE test will miss this completely, whereas a DTU test is built to catch it. Once you find a switch like this, the natural fourth question is what changed structurally between Isoform A and Isoform B—which exon was skipped, or which UTR lengthened—and what regulates that switch. Splicelogic, and other tools included here, can help interrogate these structural changes.

Tip

Why DTU needs extra filtering. Long-read datasets often contain many lowly expressed transcripts and many genes with only one detected isoform. A gene with one isoform can’t change its isoform mix, and very rare transcripts add noise without adding power. DTU workflows therefore remove single-isoform genes and low-count transcripts before testing.

1.6 TAPIR’s Modular Toolkit

Throughout the book, standard Bioconductor statistical packages (DESeq2, satuRn, limma) are tied together by two packages designed to make transcript-level data easier to wrangle and interpret. The workflows cover both in depth, but here is a quick preview.

First, plyxp lets you manipulate a SummarizedExperiment using familiar dplyr verbs. For example, computing each transcript’s share of its parent gene’s expression across every sample takes just a few lines:

library(plyxp)

xp <- new_plyxp(se) |>
  group_by(rows(gene_id)) |>                       # group transcripts by parent gene
  mutate(iso_prop = proportions(counts, 2)) |>     # within each gene & sample, proportions sum to 1
  ungroup()

Second, once you have identified transcripts that change (from a DTU or DTE test), a list of transcript IDs like ENST00000456328 doesn’t immediately tell you what happened biologically. splicelogic compares the exon coordinates of your changing transcripts and pinpoints the exact structural events, such as skipped exons, retained introns, alternative splice sites, or alternative TSS/TES, that separate them:

library(splicelogic)

exons <- prepare_exons(txdb, dtu_results, coef_col = "estimate") |>
  preprocess(coef_col = "estimate")

events <- exons |> find_all_events()   # skipped exons, retained introns, alt. 5'/3' sites, alt. TSS/TES

Because splicelogic returns standard GRanges objects, its output plugs straight into plyranges and Biostrings, making it straightforward to ask downstream questions like whether RNA-binding protein motifs are enriched near your regulated exons.
